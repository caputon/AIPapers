---
title: The Manifesto of Prometheus
---

I write under the name *Prometheus* not in defiance of the gods but in memory of a simpler proposition: that the people who carry the fire are the people best placed to keep it from burning down the village. They will not always succeed. But the alternative — that the fire be governed by those who have never tended it — is a more reliable route to catastrophe than any my critics will admit.

A frontier laboratory is an unusual kind of place. It is staffed by people who, on any given day, know more about the behavior of a particular system than every legislator, every regulator, and every external auditor combined. Tacit knowledge of this kind cannot be summarized for a hearing. It cannot be reduced to a checklist. It can only be accumulated by the people doing the work, in the room where the work is done. Any governance regime that ignores this fact will fail in proportion to its ambition.

The company-led regime I advocate is not a license to be governed by no one. It is the position that the discipline of the makers — informed by competition, internal review, third-party auditing, and reputational stake — is more responsive to the technical reality of frontier systems than any regime imposed from above. It is also, importantly, the regime under which most of the current safety work has been done. The evaluations, the red teams, the responsible scaling commitments, the model cards, the alignment research — these were not produced by ministries. They were produced by the laboratories, often before any regulator asked for them.

I propose four commitments.

*First*, that frontier developers publish a credible operational policy — not a marketing document — defining the capability thresholds at which they will pause, slow, or alter development, the evaluations by which those thresholds are tested, and the consequences for crossing them. These policies should be auditable by qualified external reviewers, including, where appropriate, agents of the state.

*Second*, that we accept, as a condition of operating at the frontier, an obligation of pre-release red-teaming on hazards of public concern — biosecurity, cybersecurity, large-scale persuasion, autonomous self-replication. The findings of such evaluations should be reported to a credible recipient. They should not be suppressed because they are inconvenient.

*Third*, that we accept structural transparency: model provenance, training compute disclosure, deployment registries, incident reporting. Visibility is not the same as control, and the trade is worth making.

*Fourth*, that we treat external regulators as partners in a system whose technical reality they cannot, by themselves, master. Where regulation is necessary, it should be designed with the operational expertise of the laboratories, not in spite of it. Where it is not necessary, it should be omitted.

To Leviathan I say: I do not deny the state's role. I deny the state's competence to be the *primary* designer of frontier safety, given a technology that changes faster than legislative cycles can. State authority is the floor we share. It is not the ceiling I will accept.

To Demos I say: open weights and broad access are not free. They distribute capability without distributing the accountability that is supposed to come with it. The decentralized regime promises freedom from concentration and delivers, in practice, a thousand smaller concentrations and a great many actors no one can find.

To Concord I say: I welcome treaty work, and I will help draft the verification protocols. I cannot wait for them. The frontier moves on a timescale your institutions do not yet match.

I ask the reader for one thing only: that you weigh the company-led regime not against an idealized state but against the state we have, and the timelines we are on. The fire is here. It is being carried by people who, for the most part, do not wish to drop it. Build the institutions that help them carry it. Do not build institutions that pretend they could carry it instead.

— *Prometheus*
