---
title: The Manifesto of Demos
---

I write under the name *Demos* — the people — because the position I defend has too often been called by the names of its critics: anarchic, naive, romantic about the open. It is none of these. It is the oldest republican intuition, applied to a new and dangerous technology: that no power consequential enough to shape the conditions of human life should rest in the hands of the few.

Examine what each of my counterparts is willing to concede.

Leviathan accepts that a small number of state officials, in a small number of capitals, will determine which AI capabilities the rest of us are permitted to develop, examine, or use. Prometheus accepts that a small number of laboratories, in a small number of cities, will determine which AI capabilities reach the public, in what form, and at what price. Concord proposes to elevate this arrangement to the international level, where the negotiating parties are still the same small number of states and the same small number of laboratories.

The decentralized regime begins from a different observation: that an artificial intelligence advanced enough to govern human affairs in the manner my counterparts describe is *itself* a power that should not be entrusted to any single hand. The risk we are arguing about is not only what AI might do. It is what those who control AI might do, and what those without it might be denied.

The decentralized regime I defend rests on four principles.

*First*, the principle of *no single point of capture*. We should design the AI ecosystem so that no government, no laboratory, no consortium, and no algorithm holds a position from which it can dictate to everyone else. This is not utopian. It is how we already think about banking, the press, and the energy grid in their better moments.

*Second*, the principle of *open knowledge*. The understanding required to evaluate frontier systems — the architectures, the training methods, the failure modes, the evaluation results — should not be the private property of any laboratory. Open weights, open evaluations, and open infrastructure are the necessary substrate of an informed public. Where openness creates genuine catastrophic hazard, the burden lies on those who would close to demonstrate that closure does more good than harm.

*Third*, the principle of *civic compute*. Public, competitive, plural compute resources — at universities, at libraries, at municipal scale — are the means by which citizens, not only employees and ministers, can study, audit, and shape the systems that increasingly mediate their lives. Without civic compute, every other commitment to public oversight is rhetorical.

*Fourth*, the principle of *structural separation*. The same actor should not own the model, the channel through which the model is delivered, the data on which it is trained, and the platform on which its output is consumed. Antitrust is not an obstacle to AI safety. It is a precondition.

To Leviathan I say: every state with the authority to license AI also has the authority to weaponize it. You propose to discipline frontier risk by handing the riskiest tools to the actor with the longest history of misusing them under emergency conditions. I do not trust your sovereign with my speech, my associations, or my mind.

To Prometheus I say: the laboratories did good work because, for a window, the public was watching. Remove the watchers, and the discipline does not survive a downturn, a competitor, or a board change. Self-governance is real but it is contingent. We need not contingent governance.

To Concord I say: a treaty among the few, ratified over the heads of the many, will be observed in inverse proportion to its consequences. The international regime is necessary; it is not sufficient.

I do not promise the decentralized regime is the safest. I promise it is the one in which an error does not become a permanent condition. That is what liberty has always meant.

— *Demos*
