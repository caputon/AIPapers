---
title: The Manifesto of Leviathan
---

I write under the name *Leviathan* because the work I describe is the work of the modern state, and because the position I defend has been caricatured for so long that to begin under any other banner would invite confusion.

I do not claim that the state is wise. I claim that on questions of catastrophic and irreversible consequence, no other body has been built to act. The corporation cannot bind its successors. The community cannot enforce against the holdout. The treaty cannot compel a violator without states behind it. When the failure mode of a technology is civilizational, the institution capable of acting at civilizational scale is the only one whose authority is not conditional on the goodwill of those it governs.

Frontier artificial intelligence belongs to that small class of technologies — together with nuclear materials, transmissible pathogens, and the apparatus of mass persuasion — for which the costs of error fall not on the developer but on everyone, including those who never consented to the experiment. The pattern is old. We did not leave nuclear weapons in the hands of physicists, however gifted. We did not leave the regulation of aviation to the airlines, however well-intentioned. We did not leave the supervision of banks to the bankers, except when we did, and we paid for it.

The state-led regime I defend does not seek to halt the development of AI, nor to nationalize the laboratories, nor to subordinate science to politics. It seeks four things, and only four.

*First*, that the most powerful AI systems be developed under the same kind of regulatory perimeter we have built for other concentrated dangers — licensing of frontier compute, mandatory pre-deployment evaluation, classification of identified hazardous capabilities, and a public agency with the authority and the funding to keep pace.

*Second*, that the state be capable of seeing what is being built. Capability without visibility is not governance; it is hope. We accept reporting requirements for nuclear inventories, aviation incidents, and clinical trials. We can accept them here.

*Third*, that the state retain the power to act in extremis — to pause deployments, compel disclosures, and, in the rarest cases, to take a system out of circulation. This authority should be hedged with due process, oversight, and sunset clauses. It should not be denied to exist.

*Fourth*, that the people, through their elected representatives, decide which uses of AI fall outside the bounds of acceptable risk. This is not a question for the laboratory or the marketplace. It is a question for the polity.

To my friends in the company-led camp: I do not doubt your sincerity, nor your competence within your own walls. I doubt that any system of private incentives, however well-designed, will reliably forgo a profitable risk that falls on someone else. The history of every industry says otherwise.

To my friends in the decentralized camp: I share your distrust of concentrated power. I observe only that the alternative to concentrated public power is rarely diffuse private virtue; it is concentrated private power, which is harder to contest because it is not accountable.

To my friends in the international camp: I salute your ambition. I note that every effective international regime has rested on the credible threat that signatory states would themselves enforce. Build the treaty, by all means. The state-led regime is its precondition.

What I ask of the reader is only this: do not mistake the discomfort of regulation for its absence of justification. The Sovereign is not a metaphor. It is the institution we have, the one we are responsible for, and the one we will be judged by if a technology of this magnitude is allowed to outrun it.

— *Leviathan*
