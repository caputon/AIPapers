---
title: The Manifesto of Concord
---

I write under the name *Concord* — the Roman *Concordia*, the goddess of agreement among parties whose interests do not naturally align. The name is deliberate. The position I defend assumes neither friendship among states nor alignment among laboratories, only the recognition that some technologies cannot be governed by any one of us, and that arrangements of mutual restraint are how civilizations have survived such technologies before.

Frontier artificial intelligence has a property that none of my counterparts can entirely accommodate within their preferred regime: it is *transboundary by construction*. A model trained in one jurisdiction is deployable in any other within hours. A capability discovered in one laboratory is, sooner or later, reproduced elsewhere. A misuse originating from one actor lands on populations far from the actor's reach. The structure of the technology does not respect borders, and a governance regime that does not address borders cannot govern the technology.

The international regime I defend has historical antecedents. The International Atomic Energy Agency was created not because nations trusted one another but because they did not, and concluded that the alternative to verification was racing in the dark. The Montreal Protocol succeeded not because firms wanted to phase out CFCs but because the regime made a coordinated phase-out cheaper than holding out. The Outer Space Treaty did not abolish national interest; it made certain forms of national interest costlier to pursue. These are the institutions I take as my model.

I propose four pillars.

*First*, a multilateral compute observatory. Frontier-scale training runs should be reportable across jurisdictions in a manner analogous to nuclear-material accounting — not to halt them, but to make their existence common knowledge among the parties whose populations they affect. Verification need not begin perfect. It needs to begin.

*Second*, a shared evaluation protocol for high-consequence capabilities. The hazards of greatest concern — autonomous cyber operations, biological uplift, large-scale autonomous influence — are hazards to all parties. Evaluations should be developed jointly, conducted under shared methodology, and findings escalated through a standing channel.

*Third*, a treaty floor on uses we agree to forgo. Every successful arms-control regime has rested on the recognition that some capabilities are worth more to all parties forsworn than wielded. The candidates are debatable; the principle is not.

*Fourth*, an institutional home — modest at first, expanding by demonstration of competence — for adjudicating disputes, accrediting evaluators, and providing a credible recipient for the disclosures my colleagues at the laboratories already make to no one in particular.

To Leviathan I say: I do not displace your sovereignty. I make it survivable. A unilateral national security regime is a regime that wins until another state declines to lose, at which point it becomes a regime that loses spectacularly.

To Prometheus I say: I do not slow your work. I provide it with a credible recipient — an institution to which your evaluations can be reported with assurance that they will be taken seriously by other states whose laboratories you cannot otherwise compel.

To Demos I say: the international regime is the friend of the decentralized regime, not its rival. A multilateral floor on the worst uses creates space below it for the plural ecosystem you defend. Without a floor, the plural ecosystem is one defection away from being closed.

I do not pretend the international regime is fast. The laboratories will move faster, and the states will lurch ahead and behind. But what is slow is not always too slow. The Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons was negotiated for years and has, however imperfectly, held for decades. It was not written when the bomb was new. It was written when the alternative had become unthinkable. That moment, with respect to AI, is not far off. We should be drafting now.

— *Concord*
