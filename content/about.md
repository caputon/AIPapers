In 1787 and 1788, the question of how a continent should be governed was argued in newspapers under Roman pseudonyms. *Publius* defended the proposed Constitution; *Brutus*, *Cato*, and the *Federal Farmer* objected. The pseudonyms were not affectations. They forced each writer to argue from first principles rather than from personality, and they let readers compare positions on equal footing.

Two centuries later, an analogous question has arrived without an analogous exchange. Advanced artificial intelligence is reshaping the conditions of national security, economic life, civic discourse, and individual liberty faster than any of our institutions can absorb. Yet the public debate about how AI should be governed remains scattered across podcasts, op-eds, and policy briefings — rarely staged in a way that makes the underlying tradeoffs visible.

**The AI Papers** is a small attempt at staging that exchange.

Four pseudonymous advocates argue for four governance regimes:

- **Leviathan**, for state-led governance — the position that catastrophic-risk technologies require sovereign authority of the kind the modern state was built to wield.
- **Prometheus**, for company-led governance — the position that the builders of the technology, disciplined by competition and accountable to internal expertise, are best positioned to steward it.
- **Demos**, for decentralized governance — the position that liberty and safety alike depend on no single hand holding so consequential a power.
- **Concord**, for international governance — the position that a planetary technology requires planetary institutions, in the spirit of the nuclear and climate regimes that came before.

For each of three organizing questions, every regime offers a primary essay; the other three then reply.

A few caveats. The essays are AI-generated. Each regime is steelmanned in its own voice, but the document is a thought experiment, not an endorsement. The set of regimes is not exhaustive — a *Pause* faction or a *Scientist-Republic* faction could plausibly join a future edition. And the questions are starting points, not the whole agenda.

The aim is modest: to make the tradeoffs visible, to let a reader walk away knowing where each regime is strongest, where it strains, and what it costs to choose one over another.
