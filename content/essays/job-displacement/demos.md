---
title: That Concentration, Not Substitution, Is the Cause of Dislocation
---

The framing of the question of work and AI has been, in the discourse of the moment, narrower than the question warrants. We are asked to consider how to handle the displacement of workers by AI, as if the displacement were a fact about the technology and the question only how to manage its consequences. I propose that the displacement, in the form we have come to fear, is not a fact about the technology. It is a fact about who controls the technology.

A capability that can be wielded by anyone — an open weight that runs on commodity hardware, a fine-tuned variant adapted to a small business, a public-domain assistant supported by civic compute — is a capability that augments labor as readily as it substitutes for labor. It is when the capability is held exclusively, deployed by the few, and licensed back to the many on terms set by its holders that the augmentation is selective and the substitution unilateral. The dislocation we fear is, in its sharpest form, the dislocation of a labor market in which capital has acquired a productive instrument that labor has not been permitted to acquire on comparable terms.

The decentralized regime addresses the question of displacement by addressing its structural cause. Where AI capability is plural, where the tools available to a multinational are also available to a small business, where a worker can deploy the same model in their own practice that the firm down the street deploys against them, the technology operates on the labor market as previous productive technologies have operated: as a force that, in time, raises the productivity of labor in concert with capital, and that distributes its gains in proportion to the broader plurality of who holds the new tools.

I do not pretend this is a complete answer. The transition will be uneven; some workers will face dislocations that no plurality of tools can prevent. The decentralized regime addresses these dislocations through public instruments — yes, including the social insurance Leviathan defends — but it locates the question of *whose tools* upstream of the question of *whose income*. To address the income question without addressing the tools question is to manage the symptoms of an arrangement whose cure is structural.

I propose four commitments.

The first is *the structural availability of AI tools to workers and small employers*. Open-weight models suitable for the great majority of professional applications should be the public default, supported by civic compute resources at the scale required to make their use practical for parties without the budgets of the largest firms. The arrangement is not a subsidy. It is the recognition that a productive instrument concentrated in the hands of a few is the precondition of the displacement we are otherwise asked to manage after the fact.

The second is *antitrust posture against vertical integration in the AI labor market*. The firms most likely to drive systematic displacement are the firms positioned to control, simultaneously, the model, the platform on which the model is deployed, and the labor market through which the model substitutes for the labor of others. The same instruments by which earlier polities prevented vertical integration in railways, broadcasting, and banking are the instruments by which the present polity may prevent vertical integration in AI labor. They are not new. They have, in domains other than AI, fallen into disuse. Their restoration is a precondition of any meaningful response to the displacement question.

The third is *worker access to the instruments of AI augmentation*, structurally protected. Where a firm deploys an AI system that materially affects the labor of its workers, the workers should have access — at a minimum, for purposes of evaluation, training, and complementary use — to the same system. This is not a workplace innovation; it is the digital analogue of long-established protections for workers' access to the tools of their trade. A worker barred from understanding the system that conditions their work is a worker rendered structurally weaker than a worker who, with the same system in hand, can compete on capability.

The fourth is *civic infrastructure for displaced workers, modeled on the public library, not the unemployment office*. The plural regime conceives of the response to displacement not primarily as income replacement — though income replacement remains necessary — but as the provision of the tools, training, and institutional support by which displaced workers can themselves deploy AI in occupations of their own choosing. Public AI infrastructure, on the model of public libraries, is the form this provision takes. The library was not designed to compensate workers for the printing press. It was designed to give them access to its products. The civic AI infrastructure I propose has the same purpose.

To Leviathan I say: the social insurance and retraining instruments you propose are necessary; I do not contest them. They are insufficient if the tools that produce the displacement remain concentrated. Build the public instruments, by all means. Build also the public infrastructure that gives workers access to the same tools the firms deploy, so the public instruments are not in perpetuity managing a displacement the structure of the technology continues to produce.

To Prometheus I say: the firms' transition financing is welcome. I observe that the firms' incentives to fund transition diminish in inverse proportion to the firms' market power. Where the displacement is producing extraordinary returns to the firm, the firm has every incentive to fund modest mitigation; where the displacement is producing modest returns and the firm is competing for them, no transition financing arrives. Voluntary commitments are a function of profit. They are not, on their own, a function of the displaced.

To Concord I say: international coordination on labor protections is necessary. The plural regime, however, locates the answer to displacement upstream of the labor regime — at the level of who controls the tools — and an international regime focused on labor protection without addressing tool concentration leaves the underlying problem in place.

The displacement we fear is not the inevitable arc of a technology operating on a labor market. It is the predictable consequence of a particular arrangement of who controls that technology. Change the arrangement, and the question of displacement becomes a smaller question — manageable by the public instruments my correspondents and I largely agree on, in proportion to the residual after the structural cure.

— *Demos*
