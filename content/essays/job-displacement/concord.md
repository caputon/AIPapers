---
title: That No Domestic Labor Regime Survives International Arbitrage
---

The question of work and AI is, in the discourse of the moment, debated almost exclusively at the domestic level. Each of my correspondents proposes a regime — public, corporate, or plural — under which the displacement of workers by frontier AI will be managed within the boundaries of a single polity. I observe that no such regime has, in any prior industrial transition, survived prolonged contact with international arbitrage on the questions it sought to address. The relevant precedents are uniformly cautionary, and the relevant lesson uniformly the same: a labor regime that does not have an international counterpart is a labor regime that holds for as long as the polity's economic position permits, and not a moment longer.

Consider the structure of the present transition through an international lens. A firm operating in a polity that has adopted the social insurance, retraining, and tax instruments Leviathan defends faces costs that a competing firm in another polity does not. A firm in a polity with strong labor protections faces costs that a firm in a polity without those protections does not. Capital — including the capital that funds the laboratories, the deployments, and the displacements — flows, in the long run, to the configuration of regulation that minimizes its costs. The polity that adopts the most thoughtful domestic regime in isolation is the polity whose firms relocate, whose labor demand softens, and whose carefully constructed instruments are starved of the tax base required to fund them.

This is not a hypothetical. It is the observed behavior of every cross-border industry over the past half-century, and there is no reason to expect frontier AI — a technology of unusual mobility, low capital intensity in deployment, and strong cross-border substitutability — to behave otherwise. A worker in a polity with thoughtful AI labor protections competes, on the same network and within the same supply chains, with AI capability deployed from a polity without them. The protections are nominally domestic; their effect on the worker's actual position is mediated by an international labor market in which the protections do not bind the relevant competitor.

The international regime I defend on this question rests on four commitments.

The first is *coordinated taxation of AI rents across jurisdictions*. The argument for taxing the extraordinary gains of frontier AI to fund transition is not, on the merits, controversial in any of the regimes my correspondents defend. The political economy of doing so domestically is, however, fragile in the absence of coordination: a polity that taxes AI rents alone produces an immediate incentive for the firms to relocate the taxable activity. The framework long developed for international taxation of multinational corporations — refined in the recent global minimum tax negotiations — is the appropriate substrate. AI rents should be taxed under a coordinated regime, with revenues distributed in part by reference to the populations bearing the displacement.

The second is *a multilateral floor on labor protections in AI-mediated work*. As frontier AI systems mediate increasing fractions of the global labor market — gig platforms, automated hiring, algorithmic management, AI-assisted task allocation — the labor protections of any individual polity become a function of whether competing polities have similar protections. A multilateral floor, modeled on the labor standards of the International Labour Organization, is the instrument by which the protections of any one polity become survivable in an international market.

The third is *international portability of training and credentialing*. A worker displaced by AI in one polity will, increasingly, retrain into work whose markets are international. Credentialing, training, and the recognition of skills should be portable across jurisdictions — not as a matter of free-trade convenience, but as a matter of the worker's standing in the international labor market frontier AI is producing. The instruments are well-precedented in the European Union and other regional integrations; their extension to the AI-affected occupations is the next chapter of the same instrument.

The fourth is *coordinated migration policy for AI-displaced workers*. The historical response to industrial dislocation has, in many cases, been migration — to the polity where the new work was emerging, on terms negotiated between the receiving and sending polities. AI-driven displacement will, at scale, occasion migration of comparable magnitude. The coordinated framework under which such migration is managed humanely, productively, and without the political destabilization of unmanaged flows, is itself an international instrument that does not yet exist in adequate form.

To Leviathan I say: I welcome the domestic instruments you defend. I observe that each is undermined, in the medium run, by the absence of a corresponding international substrate. The state-led regime is not the alternative to the international regime; it is its national chapter. A national chapter without the international book is a chapter that ends prematurely, when the firms relocate.

To Prometheus I say: the firms' commitments to transition financing are welcome and, in many domains, well-precedented. They will, in the absence of coordination, be diluted by the predictable behavior of any firm whose competitor is not bound by similar commitments. International coordination is not in tension with the firms' role; it is the substrate on which the firms' role is sustained against the pressure of arbitrage.

To Demos I say: I admire the plural regime's commitment to distributing the tools. I observe that the tools' availability in one polity does not, by itself, address the labor-market arbitrage in which workers in one polity compete with AI-augmented work performed in another. Civic compute in one polity is a virtue; civic compute as a planetary instrument, coordinated across jurisdictions, is the international form of the same commitment.

The transition we anticipate is not, in its scope, a transition the institutions of any one polity will manage alone. The history of cross-border industrial change is uniform on this point. The polities that have managed such transitions well have done so by negotiating the terms of the international labor order alongside the construction of their domestic instruments. The polities that have not have, in time, found their domestic instruments insufficient against the structural pressures of an order they did not negotiate. Concord proposes that we negotiate.

— *Concord*
