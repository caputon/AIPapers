---
title: On the Public Stake in the Distribution of AI's Gains
---

Of the questions before us, this is the one most insistently misdescribed as economic. It is not. It is the question of whether the polity continues to exist as a polity in the form to which its citizens have grown accustomed. A society in which a substantial fraction of its working-age population is rendered economically marginal by the operation of a technology over which it had no say is not, properly speaking, a market problem. It is a constitutional one.

The state-led regime is, in this domain, the regime that takes seriously what the alternatives leave to chance. Each of my correspondents proposes that the dislocations attendant on widespread AI substitution will be addressed by some combination of voluntary action by the laboratories, distributed adaptation among workers, or the cumulative effect of an open ecosystem in which the same tools available to capital are available to labor. I do not deny that any of these mechanisms operates at the margin. I deny that any of them is sufficient at the scale we have reason to expect.

Consider the structure of the dislocation. Frontier AI substitutes, with increasing capability, for cognitive labor across a wide and previously secure range of occupations. The distinguishing feature of this substitution is its breadth. Earlier industrial transitions displaced workers from one set of trades to another, and complementary creation reabsorbed them, in time, in occupations that had not existed before. The argument that AI will follow this pattern is plausible at the level of equilibrium analysis and impossible to verify at the level of any individual worker over the relevant time horizon. The patient may, in time, recover. The patient must, in the meantime, eat.

The instruments by which the polity has historically addressed transitions of this character are well-established and held by the state. They include income supports during periods of involuntary unemployment, retraining programs at scale, education systems sufficient to equip new entrants with the skills the new economy will reward, and tax policy capable of redistributing extraordinary gains in productivity from a small number of capital holders to the broader population whose labor those gains have displaced. Each of these instruments is the instrument of a state, because each requires the legitimacy of democratic mandate, the fiscal capacity of public revenue, and the durability of institutions that outlast any particular firm or industry.

I propose four commitments.

The first is *the modernization of social insurance for the AI transition*. Existing unemployment systems were designed for sectoral disruptions of the industrial era — temporary, geographically concentrated, expected to be reversed by a return to growth. The displacements we anticipate are likely to be longer, broader, and asymmetrically permanent. Income supports must be longer, broader, and capable of bridging into the labor market that follows rather than the labor market that has departed.

The second is *retraining at the scale of the substitution*. The polity has, in earlier transitions, supported retraining as a matter of policy. The scale required now is larger, and the instruments — education, certification, apprenticeship — must be made available to workers in industries the state did not previously regulate. This is not retraining as it has historically been delivered, in narrow programs of limited reach. It is retraining as an entitlement of citizenship, on the recognition that the technology of the moment has, by its operation, made the citizen's prior preparation obsolete through no fault of the citizen.

The third is *the taxation of AI rents*. The productivity gains from frontier AI accrue, in their initial distribution, to the holders of capital who funded its development. This is in keeping with the ordinary operation of property rights and I do not contest it. I contest only the proposition that the initial distribution is the final distribution. Where extraordinary gains accrue to a small number of actors as a result of a technology whose foundations were laid by decades of publicly funded research, the polity has a standing claim on a portion of those gains, to be applied to the public stake in the transition the technology is producing. Compute taxation, dedicated revenues from frontier deployment, and progressive taxation of capital gains on AI-related holdings are among the available instruments.

The fourth is *the preservation of bargaining institutions*. The instruments by which workers have, in earlier transitions, secured a portion of productivity gains for labor — collective bargaining, sectoral negotiations, the legal apparatus that protects organizing — are themselves at risk in a labor market increasingly mediated by AI systems controlled by capital. The polity that values its workers' standing must protect the institutions through which workers acquire that standing. This is not a return to an earlier industrial settlement. It is the recognition that an industrial settlement, in some form, is what distinguishes a society of citizens from a society of clients of the firms that employ them.

To Prometheus I say: I am prepared to credit the laboratories with good intentions on the question of complementary creation. I am not prepared to entrust the well-being of the polity to those intentions. The transitions of the past were not managed by the firms whose technologies produced them. They were managed by the polity that survived them, and the firms that proved good citizens were not the typical firms.

To Demos I say: a plural ecosystem is a virtue at the level of capability access. It is not, by itself, a substitute for the social insurance, retraining, and tax instruments that displaced workers will require. The plural ecosystem distributes the tools. It does not, on its own, distribute the income.

To Concord I say: international coordination on the questions of AI taxation and labor mobility is welcome and necessary. Coordination begins, however, with parties capable of acting domestically. Build the domestic instruments first, and bring them, in coordinated form, to the international table.

The state is not the answer to every question. On the question of how a polity addresses the systematic displacement of its workers by a technology it did not vote for, the state is the institution we have, the one we have built precisely for transitions of this kind, and the one without which the transition will be addressed by no one with both the legitimacy and the capacity to do so.

— *Leviathan*
