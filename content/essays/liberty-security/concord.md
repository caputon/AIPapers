---
title: That Liberty Within a State Is Inseparable from Order Among States
---

The defenders of the state-led, company-led, and decentralized regimes all share an unspoken assumption: that the question of liberty and security under frontier AI is, fundamentally, a question to be answered within the boundaries of a single political community. Leviathan addresses the citizens of the polity. Prometheus addresses the relationship between the laboratory and its public. Demos addresses the citizen and the technology. None addresses the structural fact that frontier AI is a transboundary technology, and that liberty and security defined for one polity, in isolation, are now meaningless without an account of what happens when other polities make different choices.

I propose, as the international advocate, that the question of liberty and security under frontier AI cannot be answered well within a single jurisdiction. It must be answered in concert, or it will be answered for us by whichever jurisdiction is least inclined to ask it.

Consider the situation of a state that adopts the most thoughtful version of any of the three regimes my correspondents defend. Suppose Leviathan's regime is built — licensure, classification, pre-deployment review, the apparatus complete. Suppose Prometheus's regime is layered atop it — credible operational policies at the laboratories, external audits, transparency at the level of compute and deployment. Suppose Demos's regime is woven through it — open weights for non-hazardous systems, civic compute, antitrust posture against vertical concentration. The polity has done well. It has the apparatus of liberty and the apparatus of security. It has, in the language of an older debate, secured itself.

Now suppose another polity — its laboratories, its citizens, its government — has not. Suppose, on the contrary, that another polity is racing, with looser controls, narrower transparency, and a state apparatus less inclined to mediate between national interest and frontier capability. The capabilities developed in that polity will arrive, sooner or later, in ours. They will arrive over the network. They will arrive through the ordinary channels by which information crosses borders today. They will arrive in a form that the most capable domestic regime cannot prevent and may not be able to detect. The liberty and security painstakingly constructed within the first polity will, at the moment of arrival, become a unilateral disarmament — a regime that has constrained its own actors and protected its own citizens against catastrophes that now reach them through a door no domestic regulator was empowered to close.

This is not a hypothetical. It is the structural condition of every transboundary technology — the condition that produced the IAEA, the Montreal Protocol, the Outer Space Treaty, the BWC, and every other instrument by which the community of nations has, with mixed success, attempted to govern capabilities that exceed the reach of any single state. The history of these regimes is not a history of perfect cooperation. It is a history of imperfect cooperation that, on the relevant questions, was less imperfect than the unilateral alternative.

The international regime I defend rests on four propositions.

The first is that *the most consequential AI capabilities are, by their nature, of common concern* to the community of nations, and that the question of how to govern them is, by their nature, a question on which states must speak to one another or risk being spoken to by the technology itself. This is not the same as a global government. It is the recognition that some capabilities, like nuclear materials and transmissible pathogens, exceed the proper scope of any single jurisdiction's monopoly on response.

The second is that *the institutional substrate of an international regime can be built before the underlying agreements are fully negotiated*. A multilateral compute observatory — modest at first, modeled on the safeguards regime of the IAEA — does not require all parties to have agreed in advance on what they will and will not develop. It requires only that they agree to make their development legible to one another. Legibility is the foundation on which subsequent agreement is built. Without it, agreement is an aspiration.

The third is that *shared evaluation protocols are within reach today*. The hazards of greatest international concern — biological uplift, autonomous cyber operations of strategic scale, large-scale autonomous influence — are hazards on which there is broader technical consensus across the community of states than there is on the politics of who should govern the laboratories. Begin with the technical consensus. The political consensus follows when the technical work is credible.

The fourth is that *a treaty floor on uses agreed to be forsworn* is the historical instrument by which civilization has, repeatedly, prevented the worst use of a powerful technology. The Biological Weapons Convention is imperfect. It is, nonetheless, the reason there is no biological weapons industry. The Outer Space Treaty is imperfect. It is, nonetheless, the reason there are no nuclear weapons in orbit. The Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons is imperfect. It is, nonetheless, the reason that nine states have nuclear weapons rather than fifty. The international regime I defend rests on the historically attested observation that imperfect treaty regimes, in domains of catastrophic risk, outperform the absence of treaty regimes by margins that should command the attention of any serious reader.

The international regime is not a substitute for the regimes my correspondents defend. It is the condition under which their regimes' liberty and security commitments retain their meaning across the borders that the technology already crosses.

To Leviathan I say: every domestic licensing regime requires an international verification regime to prevent its becoming a regime of unilateral disadvantage. Build the licensure. Build, also, the protocols by which other states can credibly observe that you have built the licensure, and you can credibly observe that they have done the same.

To Prometheus I say: every operational commitment by a frontier developer requires a credible international recipient to whom the commitment can be reported, evaluated, and held against. Without that recipient, the commitment is a contract with no enforcement, in a sector where the parties competent to enforce do not yet sit at the same table.

To Demos I say: the international regime is not the friend of the great powers against the public. It is the only mechanism by which the worst uses of AI by the great powers can be constrained at all, and the only mechanism by which a plural ecosystem can be defended against the proliferation of capabilities that would otherwise drive the ecosystem toward closure.

The Roman *Concordia* did not promise that the parties to her temple loved one another. She promised only that, at certain moments, on certain questions, they would behave as if they did, because the alternative was unsurvivable. We approach such a moment now.

— *Concord*
