---
title: That Safety Is the Practice of the Builders, and Liberty Its Necessary Condition
---

I begin with a fact that the proponents of state-led governance pass over too quickly: most of the substantive safety work on frontier AI to date has been performed inside the laboratories themselves. The capability evaluations, the responsible scaling commitments, the model cards, the deployment policies, the alignment research, the red-teaming protocols, the dangerous-capability tests — these are not artifacts of a regulatory state that has yet to come into being. They are products of the institutions Leviathan would now bring under closer supervision.

This is not an accident, and it is not a temporary feature of an immature industry. It is a structural fact about frontier systems: they are evaluated, refined, and made safer by people in continuous contact with the systems themselves. Tacit knowledge of the kind required to recognize emergent risks cannot be specified in advance, communicated through hearings, or codified in regulations promulgated quarterly. It accumulates inside the laboratories. To displace the laboratories from the center of the safety effort, in pursuit of an apparatus that purports to do the same work from outside, is to weaken the very capability we depend on. Liberty, on this view, is not the price we pay for safety. Liberty — the operational latitude to do the work — is the condition under which safety is produced.

Permit me, then, to describe what the company-led regime asks for, what it does not ask for, and why it represents the more reliable approach to the question of liberty and security under frontier AI.

The company-led regime asks, first, for *operational accountability* rather than prescriptive control. A laboratory operating at the frontier should publish, and be held to, a written commitment specifying the capability thresholds at which it will pause, slow, or alter its development trajectory. These are not aspirations. They are decision rules. They identify the evaluations by which thresholds are tested, the responses required when thresholds are crossed, and the conditions under which the laboratory accepts external review. Such commitments exist today, in varying forms, at the major frontier developers. The question before us is not whether to invent them. It is whether to subject them to credible audit, harden them against backsliding, and extend them to actors who have not yet adopted them.

The regime asks, second, for *external evaluation as a structural feature of frontier development*, not as a courtesy. Pre-release evaluation against hazards of broad public concern — biosecurity, cybersecurity, large-scale autonomous influence, autonomous self-replication and propagation — should be performed before deployment, by parties with the standing and competence to make findings stick. This includes qualified third-party evaluators, academic institutions, and, where warranted, agents of the state. The relevant question about the state's role is not whether the state should be involved. It is what the state should be asked to do that it can do better than the alternatives, and what it should be asked to do because no one else has democratic standing to do it.

The regime asks, third, for *transparency at the level that matters*: model provenance, training compute, deployment registries, post-deployment incident reporting. These are the inputs to a credible system of accountability. They are not the same as opening the black box, and they should not be confused with the demand that every model be inspectable by every party. Transparency that is impossible to operationalize is transparency that exists only as a slogan.

The regime asks, fourth, for *partnership with the state, not displacement by it*. Where regulation is necessary — and there are domains where it plainly is, including export controls on identified hazards and licensing of facilities operating above defined thresholds — it should be designed in concert with the technical community whose work it governs, with provisions for periodic revision as the technology evolves. Where regulation would only freeze a moving target, it should be omitted, and the work entrusted to the parties capable of moving with the target.

What the regime does not ask for is what its critics most often impute to it: a license to operate without scrutiny. Concentrated capability paired with concentrated discretion is no friend of any company-led regime worthy of the name. The laboratories I would defend are those whose internal cultures make them genuinely uncomfortable when they encounter a capability they cannot explain — and whose external commitments are robust enough that this discomfort cannot be quietly resolved by shipping anyway.

The state-led regime, in its more ambitious versions, supposes that authority concentrated in a public agency will be exercised in the public interest by people of probity, with attention to the technical reality of the systems they regulate, on timescales that do not unduly delay the public's access to beneficial applications. I admire the supposition. I observe that the agency in question does not yet exist; that the agencies most often offered as analogies were built over decades, in adjacent industries, and perform their function imperfectly even there; and that the cost of an underpowered, underfunded, or politically captured AI agency is not zero. It is the cost of a regime that has the appearance of safety and the substance of capture.

The decentralized regime I admire, but it under-prices the genuine asymmetry between capability and accountability at the frontier. There is a window during which a capability can do harm before its broad availability creates the corrective feedback Demos relies upon. In that window, the institutions that built the capability are the institutions best positioned to act. The international regime I welcome, but its instruments mature on a timescale that the technology will not respect. Treaty work should proceed. So should everything else.

The question of liberty and security under frontier AI does not have a clean answer. It has a least-worst answer. The least-worst answer is to entrust the practice of safety to the parties capable of practicing it, to discipline that practice through credible commitments and external audit, to build the public institutions that complement rather than supplant the laboratories' work, and to refuse the invitation to mistake the appearance of regulation for the substance of safety. That is what the company-led regime offers. It is more, not less, than the alternatives.

— *Prometheus*
