---
title: On the Necessity of Sovereign Authority over Frontier Compute
---

The objection most often raised against the state-led regime is that it must compromise liberty to secure us against frontier AI risks, and that the compromise is too steep. I propose to invert the question. If liberty is the condition under which a free people may pursue their ends without arbitrary interference, then liberty has always presupposed a security against catastrophe sufficient to make the pursuit meaningful. There is no liberty to speak of in a polity whose biotechnology, financial system, or critical infrastructure can be brought down by a single weekend's misuse of a system no public institution had the authority to examine.

The Sovereign — by which I mean the constitutional state, accountable to its citizens through the ordinary instruments of representation and law — is not the enemy of liberty in this matter. It is the precondition of liberty's persistence under a technology that has otherwise placed catastrophe within the means of the few.

Consider the structural problem. A frontier laboratory today possesses, within its walls, a class of capabilities whose downside risks fall disproportionately on populations that did not consent to their creation. The internal incentives to mitigate those risks are real, but they are incomplete. Where a hazard is identified that is expensive to remediate and unlikely to be discovered, the laboratory's investors will not always vote to remediate. Where a hazard is identified that requires sharing information with competitors, the laboratory's strategic interest will not always permit it to share. Where a hazard is identified that, if disclosed, would invite the very regulation the laboratory wishes to avoid, the laboratory's lawyers will not always counsel disclosure. None of this is unique to AI. It is the ordinary failure mode of private actors confronting catastrophic externalities, and it is the reason every other technology of comparable danger sits today within a public regulatory perimeter.

The sovereign authority I defend rests on four powers, hedged by four limits.

The first power is *licensure*. A laboratory training a model above a defined compute or capability threshold should obtain authorization from a public agency, on conditions that include ongoing reporting, evaluation, and inspection. The threshold should be set high enough that ordinary research and small-scale development are unaffected; it should be set low enough that no actor at the frontier escapes oversight by structural accident. The corresponding limit is that licensure must be objective, reviewable, and unbiased among similarly situated firms — never an instrument of industrial policy in disguise.

The second power is *classification*. Specific identified capabilities — uplift to biological catastrophe, automation of cyber operations of strategic scale, autonomous self-replication — should be designated as restricted, with controls on their further development, dissemination, and deployment that resemble the controls we already place on dual-use materials. The corresponding limit is that classification must be narrow, technically grounded, and subject to challenge in court. The history of overbroad classification is not a history I wish to repeat.

The third power is *pre-deployment review*. The most consequential models, before deployment, should be evaluated against a standardized set of hazard tests — by the developer, by independent reviewers, and, where warranted, by the agency itself. Findings of significant hazard should trigger remediation before deployment, not after. The corresponding limit is that review must be timely. A regime whose review takes longer than a model's relevance is not a regime; it is an obstacle.

The fourth power is *interruption*. In genuine and identified emergencies — a model that has been demonstrated to confer catastrophic uplift, an incident that reveals an unanticipated failure mode — the agency should retain the authority to pause, recall, or restrict the deployment of a system. The corresponding limit is that interruption must be invokable only on findings of fact, subject to expedited judicial review, and accompanied by sunset provisions. Emergency authority that does not sunset is no longer emergency authority.

I make no pretense that this regime is without cost. It will slow some developments. It will inhibit some valuable applications at the margin. It will require the construction of public capacity that does not yet exist, at a moment when public capacity is mistrusted in many quarters for reasons that are not always unjust. These costs are real. They are smaller, by orders of magnitude, than the costs that any one of the catastrophic capabilities, if realized in the absence of public oversight, would impose on populations who never asked to bear them.

To those who say this regime is incompatible with the speed of innovation: I observe that pharmaceuticals, civil aviation, and nuclear energy are all subject to comparable regimes, and that these are among the most innovative sectors of the modern economy. Innovation under regulation is not innovation prevented. It is innovation directed.

To those who say the state cannot keep pace with frontier capabilities: I agree that the state cannot keep pace by itself. That is why the regime I defend incorporates the laboratories' own evaluations as a foundation, the academic community's findings as a check, and the international community's protocols as a multiplier. The Sovereign is not the only governor of frontier AI. It is the institution that ensures the others can be relied upon when relying on them most matters.

To those who say liberty is endangered by an empowered AI agency: I share the concern, and I name the limits above precisely because of it. An agency without limits is tyranny. An agency without authority is theatre. The republic has built agencies of this kind before — the Atomic Energy Commission, the Federal Aviation Administration, the Securities and Exchange Commission. Each was contested at its founding. Each has been imperfect in operation. None has been replaced by a private successor capable of doing the same work.

The question is not whether to compromise liberty. The question is which compromises preserve a polity in which liberty remains a meaningful description of how its citizens live. A frontier AI regime without sovereign authority is not a regime that preserves liberty. It is a regime that postpones the question until someone else answers it for us.

— *Leviathan*
