---
title: That Liberty and Security Are Indivisible, and Both Endangered by Concentration
---

Both of my correspondents have written ably, and both have begun from a premise I deny. They presume that liberty and security stand in tension — that the practical question is how much of the one to surrender for how much of the other, and that their disagreement concerns only who is best placed to administer the trade. I observe that both regimes, in arguing thus, take for granted that AI capability will continue to concentrate in a small number of hands. From this premise, the question of who holds the lever follows naturally; and from a small set of candidates, one must be chosen. I propose, with the respect due to my friends, that the premise itself is the problem.

The decentralized position is not that liberty matters more than security. It is that liberty *and* security depend, equally and indivisibly, on the structural condition that no single hand wields AI against the public. Where capability is concentrated, both liberty and security are endangered — by the same actors, through the same mechanisms, with the same kinds of consequences for those without standing to object. Where capability is plural, both are more nearly secured.

Consider, first, the failure modes of concentration as a threat to liberty.

A handful of laboratories, having trained the most capable systems, become the gatekeepers of access to capabilities on which the rest of society increasingly depends. Their terms of service govern what may be asked, what may be answered, what may be remembered, and what may be forwarded to whom. The economic concentration becomes, almost as a side-effect, an editorial concentration. A handful of states, having taken regulatory authority over those laboratories, acquire the power to direct, through formal and informal channels, what the laboratories do — including, if it suits the state's purposes, what the laboratories' systems will and will not do for the citizens. Liberty, in such a regime, is whatever residue is left over after the laboratory and the state have agreed.

Consider, next, the failure modes of concentration as a threat to security.

A capability concentrated in a single actor is not, by virtue of its concentration, safer than the same capability distributed broadly. It is differently dangerous. The single-actor failure mode is the catastrophic insider event, the misaligned objective, the adversarial state that compromises the laboratory's controls, the political instruction that overrides the laboratory's judgment. These are not hypothetical risks. They are the historical risks of every other technology that has ever been concentrated. The proponents of the state-led and company-led regimes assume the actor at the center of the system will act in good faith, with competence, on the public's behalf. I have read enough history to be wary of the assumption, and enough current events to be wary of it now.

The decentralized regime I propose addresses both failure modes simultaneously, through four structural choices.

The first is *the deliberate prevention of single points of capture*. The AI ecosystem should be designed so that no single laboratory, no single state, no single platform, and no single algorithm holds a position from which it can dictate to the rest. This is achieved not by sentiment but by structure: open weights for systems that do not pose identified catastrophic hazard; structural separation between model developers and the channels through which models are delivered; antitrust enforcement against vertical integration that would otherwise lock the ecosystem into a single architecture; and refusal — at the level of policy — to license configurations of compute, data, and distribution that would render any one actor too consequential to challenge.

The second is *open knowledge as the default*. The understanding required to evaluate frontier systems must not be the private possession of those with a financial interest in the conclusions. Open evaluations, open benchmarks, open architectures, and open infrastructure are the substrate of credible accountability. Where openness in a particular domain creates genuine catastrophic hazard — and there are such domains, narrowly defined — the burden lies on those who would close to demonstrate, on the record, that the closure does more good than harm. The default direction of motion is toward openness; the exceptions are exceptions.

The third is *civic compute*. Public, plural, competitively governed compute resources — at universities, at libraries, at municipalities, at any institution capable of standing for the public's interest — are the means by which citizens, not only employees and ministers, can study, audit, and shape the systems that shape them. Without civic compute, every other commitment to public oversight is a commitment to depend on the cooperation of the actor being overseen.

The fourth is *transparency by default in the deployment of AI by powerful actors*, public and private. Where AI is being used to allocate resources, screen applicants, conduct surveillance, generate persuasive content at scale, or condition access to public services, the public has a standing interest in the operation of the system. Transparency obligations should fall most heavily on the most consequential applications, and most lightly on the experimentation and use that constitutes the everyday work of free citizens with their own tools.

To the objection that the decentralized regime makes catastrophic capability available to bad actors: I answer that catastrophic capability is, in any regime, available to bad actors who are determined enough; and that the decentralized regime, by making the capability also available to defenders — to security researchers, civic technologists, public-interest organizations, and informed citizens — produces a more resilient overall posture than the alternative. A defended commons outperforms a fortified citadel that, when breached, leaves the population without recourse.

To the objection that the decentralized regime cannot manage genuine extinction-level risks: I answer that I do not pretend it can manage every risk by itself. There are narrow domains — concrete, identifiable, technically grounded — in which restrictions on dissemination are warranted, and the decentralized regime should adopt them. The point is that such restrictions should be the exception, narrowly drawn, on a record, with sunsets. The point is not that no restriction is ever justified. The point is that the default condition of an AI ecosystem compatible with a free society is plurality.

A liberty that depends on the goodwill of a small number of laboratories or a small number of agencies is not liberty. It is a privilege held at the discretion of others. A security that requires the same is not security. It is hostage-taking under a more polite name. The decentralized regime offers neither perfect liberty nor perfect security. It offers the only configuration in which the two are not held by someone else.

— *Demos*
