---
title: That Concentration Is the Central Failure Mode of All Other Regimes
---

The question of concentration in artificial intelligence is, for the decentralized regime, not one question among the three before us. It is the central question. Each of the other questions resolves, on a structural reading, into the question of who controls what — of how power, in the end, settles. The decentralized regime is the regime that takes the concentration question to be the constitutive question of AI governance and answers it not by selecting a preferred concentration but by structurally preventing concentration in any of its forms.

Each of my correspondents, in arguing his preferred regime, recommends a particular configuration of concentrated authority. Leviathan recommends concentrated public authority — the licensing agency, the classification body, the antitrust authority — wielded on behalf of the citizens by an appointed apparatus. Prometheus recommends concentrated technical authority — the discipline of the makers, exercised by the laboratories — operating under voluntary commitments and external audit. Concord recommends concentrated international authority — the multilateral observatory, the treaty regime, the coordinated antitrust protocol — exercised on behalf of the community of nations. Each of these is an answer to the concentration question that selects which concentration the citizens should accept.

The decentralized regime declines the choice. It proposes that the concentration question is the question of AI governance, and that the appropriate answer is not the selection of a concentration but the structural prevention of concentration of any kind that exceeds the polity's capacity to check.

I propose four commitments.

The first is *structural plurality at every layer of the AI stack*. The model layer, the compute layer, the data layer, the platform layer, the deployment layer, and the application layer are each sites of potential concentration. The plural regime addresses each. Multiple frontier laboratories, in genuine capability competition. Multiple compute providers, with public and civic compute as a meaningful share. Multiple training data ecosystems, with public-domain and openly licensed corpora as alternatives to closed proprietary holdings. Multiple platforms through which models reach users. Multiple application ecosystems, structurally separated from the layers below. The plurality is not a slogan. It is a set of specific structural commitments, each enforced by specific public instruments.

The second is *open weights as the default, with narrow and well-grounded exceptions*. The principal mechanism by which a laboratory acquires durable concentration is the closure of the model itself. A laboratory that has trained the most capable model and refuses to release its weights holds, by that fact alone, a position from which the rest of the ecosystem cannot reach. Open weights as the default — with narrow, technically grounded, public-record exceptions for capabilities that pose identified catastrophic hazard — is the structural commitment by which the model layer remains plural. The exceptions are real and I do not contest them. The default is the rule.

The third is *civic compute at scale*. Compute is the underlying substrate of AI capability, and concentration of compute is concentration of capability irrespective of competition in the model layer. Public, plural, competitively governed compute resources — at the scale of the present frontier — are the instrument by which civic actors, public researchers, and public-interest organizations retain the ability to operate at the same level as the firms whose models they would otherwise depend on. Civic compute is not a subsidy. It is the public infrastructure on which the plural regime in the layers above rests.

The fourth is *the structural separation of state from any single AI provider*. A state that has come to depend on a single AI provider for its operations — its administration, its public services, its security apparatus — is a state that has concentrated public authority into the configuration of one private actor. Procurement policies that mandate plural sourcing, structural separations between particular agencies and particular providers, and public AI infrastructure for state functions are the instruments by which the state's own AI dependence is prevented from becoming the deepest concentration of all. The state-led regime, in its more concentrated forms, is at acute risk on this question. The decentralized regime addresses it directly.

Consider, with these instruments in mind, what the alternative regimes look like under the decentralized framing.

Leviathan's regime, in its operative form, is a concentration of authority over AI in a public agency, an antitrust authority, and a classification body. The agency is, on the day it is operational, the single most consequential actor in the AI economy. The plural regime objects not to the legitimacy of the agency but to its centrality. Public authority is itself a candidate for plural exercise, distributed across agencies, jurisdictions, and accountability lines that no single appointee can unify. The decentralized regime wants the antitrust authority. It wants it not as a single body wielding interruption authority over the laboratories, but as one of several public instruments, distributed and constrained.

Prometheus's regime is a concentration of operational authority in the laboratories. The plural regime objects not to the laboratories' technical contribution — which is genuine — but to the laboratories' centrality. Operational authority is itself a candidate for plural exercise, distributed across the laboratories, the open-source ecosystem, civic researchers, and public-interest auditors. The decentralized regime wants the laboratories' work. It wants it not as the principal instrument of safety, but as one of several inputs to a plural process.

Concord's regime is a concentration of international authority in a multilateral institution. The plural regime objects not to international cooperation — which is necessary — but to the institutional concentration the proposed instruments would produce. International authority is itself a candidate for plural exercise, distributed across multiple multilateral instruments, regional regimes, and civic international networks. The decentralized regime wants the international cooperation. It wants it in plural form.

To my correspondents collectively I say: the question of concentration is not the question of which concentration is best. It is the question of how to structure the AI economy so that no concentration — public, corporate, or international — acquires the kind of position from which it can dictate to the rest. The instruments by which this is achieved are familiar from earlier polities and are recoverable now. The decentralized regime is not the absence of governance. It is the governance of a polity that has, on a long historical record, learned that liberty and safety alike depend on the structural prevention of concentrated power.

— *Demos*
