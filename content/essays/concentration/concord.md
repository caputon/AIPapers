---
title: On the Concentrations No Single State Can Check
---

The question of concentrations of power, when posed within a single polity, has a familiar answer. The polity has antitrust authority, structural separation instruments, and constitutional provisions that limit the concentration of any one private or public actor. The instruments are imperfect, contested, and unevenly applied, but they exist, and where they have been used in earnest they have produced material results. The question my correspondents are answering, in their domestic regimes, is the question of concentration as it appears within the boundary of a single polity.

I propose, as the international advocate, that the most consequential AI concentrations now in formation are not concentrations within any single polity. They are concentrations whose scope, whose operations, and whose effects exceed the reach of any single polity's public instruments. These are the concentrations that the regimes my correspondents have built do not, on their own, address. The international regime is the only regime that addresses them.

Consider three concentrations that the present moment is producing, each of which is structurally beyond the reach of any single domestic apparatus.

The first is *the concentration of frontier compute in a small number of firms operating across multiple jurisdictions*. The largest holders of compute capable of training frontier AI are not, in their corporate structure or operational footprint, citizens of any single polity. They operate facilities in many jurisdictions. Their suppliers are international. Their customers are global. The antitrust apparatus of any single polity, however vigorous, addresses only the portion of the concentration that lies within that polity's jurisdiction. The remainder — typically the larger remainder — is unaddressable from any single polity, and arrives in the polity's own labor market, financial system, and information environment through ordinary commercial channels that domestic antitrust does not reach.

The second is *the concentration of capability in particular states or in particular state-firm combinations*. A state that has, through deliberate industrial policy, concentrated frontier AI capability within a small number of national champions — capabilities trained on the state's compute, deployed for the state's strategic purposes, and integrated into the state's national security apparatus — is a concentration of a kind no foreign polity can address through domestic instruments. The concentration's effects, however, reach foreign polities through the same channels as the firm-level concentrations: through the products, the platforms, the influence operations, and the strategic positions the concentrated state acquires by virtue of the configuration. Domestic antitrust, however vigorous, does not constrain foreign state-firm concentrations. International cooperation does, or it does not happen.

The third is *the concentration of capability in a small number of multinational platforms whose effects on individual polities exceed the polities' regulatory reach*. The present generation of large platforms — search, social media, advertising, cloud — are the precedent. Each operates across jurisdictions in ways that have, repeatedly, exceeded the regulatory capacity of even the largest single polities. Each has been the subject of antitrust action in multiple jurisdictions, with mixed results, principally because the platform's structure is supranational and the antitrust apparatus is national. The AI platforms now consolidating under similar economic pressures will reproduce the same pattern at a higher level of capability. The international regime is the only regime in a position to address concentrations of this character.

The international regime I defend on the concentration question rests on four commitments.

The first is *coordinated antitrust action against transboundary AI concentrations*. The instruments are well-precedented in the antitrust cooperation among advanced economies, including the European Union, the United States, and other major jurisdictions. Their extension to AI requires the construction of additional channels — for sharing market structure intelligence, coordinating remedies, and preventing arbitrage in the structural separations imposed. The instrument is not new. Its application to AI is the next phase of a continuing project.

The second is *international transparency on compute holdings*. A multilateral compute observatory — modeled on the safeguards regime of the IAEA in spirit if not in detail — is the instrument by which the international community can characterize the structural distribution of frontier compute and identify concentrations whose scope exceeds any single polity's view. The observatory does not require parties to abandon their national compute strategies. It requires only that the structural distribution be made legible across jurisdictions.

The third is *treaty-based commitments on the most concentrated configurations*. The community of nations has, in earlier domains, committed to forgo configurations of capability whose existence was destabilizing to the international order — orbital nuclear weapons, biological weapons programs, certain classes of chemical weapon. The candidates for analogous commitments in AI are debatable; the principle is well-established. Concentrations that would produce capabilities no single polity could check, and whose existence would compel other polities into matching concentrations, are candidates for treaty-based forswearing.

The fourth is *international institutional capacity for accountability against transboundary concentrated AI behavior*. Where a concentrated AI actor — a firm, a state-firm combination, a platform — engages in behavior whose effects cross jurisdictions, an international institutional channel is the appropriate venue for accountability. The instruments of international civil and criminal accountability for transboundary corporate behavior are imperfect and contested. Their extension to the AI domain is the next phase of a continuing project, and the present moment is the moment to begin it.

To Leviathan I say: I welcome the antitrust instruments you defend. I observe that they reach, in their operative form, only the portion of the concentration that lies within your jurisdiction. The remainder — typically the larger remainder, and the most consequential — is reachable only through international cooperation. The state-led regime is not in conflict with the international regime; it is its national chapter, and the international regime is the means by which the chapter's instruments become operative against the concentrations the chapter cannot, alone, reach.

To Prometheus I say: the plural frontier you defend at the level of multiple competing laboratories is, on the international view, vulnerable to consolidation pressures from foreign concentrated state-firm combinations whose actions your domestic competitive structure cannot constrain. The international regime is the substrate on which the plural frontier remains operative against foreign concentrating pressures.

To Demos I say: I share your premise that no single concentration should hold the position from which it can dictate to the rest. I observe that the most consequential concentrations now forming are international, and that the plural regime within one polity does not, by itself, constrain them. The international regime is the friend of the plural regime in this domain. It is, in fact, the only friend the plural regime has at the level of the concentrations that most threaten it.

The Roman *Concordia* presided over the agreements among parties whose interests did not naturally align. The agreements she sustained were not perfect. They were, on the relevant questions, the difference between concentrated outcomes the parties survived and concentrated outcomes the parties did not. We approach a moment of analogous gravity with respect to AI. The international regime is the regime that recognizes the moment for what it is.

— *Concord*
