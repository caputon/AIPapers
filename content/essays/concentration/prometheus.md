---
title: On the Plurality of Frontiers, and the Discipline That Sustains It
---

The criticism most often directed at the company-led regime, on the question of concentration, is that it presupposes a competitive structure that the economics of frontier AI will not permanently sustain. The criticism is serious and I take it seriously. The defense of the company-led regime on this question is not that consolidation is impossible. It is that the appropriate response to consolidation is the maintenance of plural frontiers — multiple laboratories operating at the technical edge, in genuine capability competition with one another — and that this maintenance is achievable through a combination of competition policy, structural commitments, and the operational discipline of the laboratories themselves, without resort to the heavier instruments my correspondents prefer.

Begin with an observation that the public discussion has, in my view, underweighted: the present configuration of frontier AI development is, by the standards of advanced technology industries, *unusually plural*. Multiple laboratories, in multiple jurisdictions, with substantial capability differentiation, are simultaneously operating at the frontier. The investment costs are large but not so large as to have foreclosed entry by well-capitalized newcomers. The talent market remains genuinely competitive across the laboratories. Open-source contributions, while not always at the frontier, are converging on it in many domains within months of the closed releases. This is not the picture of an industry consolidating to monopoly. It is the picture of an industry whose competitive structure remains contestable.

The relevant question is not whether consolidation is theoretically possible. It is whether the present plural configuration is sustainable, and on what conditions. The company-led regime I defend supplies four conditions.

The first is *the maintenance of multiple frontier laboratories as a structural goal*, against which competition policy is directed. The polity should not, in its competition enforcement, treat AI as if it were a single market. It should treat it as a stack of related markets — model development, deployment platform, compute, training data, application layer — and direct competition policy at the vertical and horizontal concentrations that would foreclose the plural configuration. This is not the more aggressive antitrust posture Leviathan defends. It is the structural antitrust posture that targets the configurations most likely to produce non-contestable concentration, with the lighter touch the rest of the stack permits.

The second is *credible commitments by the frontier laboratories to operational openness in domains that do not implicate identified catastrophic hazard*. The principal mechanism by which closed laboratories drift toward concentration is the accumulation of operational advantages that are invisible to competitors and to the public. Where the laboratories commit to publishing the methods of their evaluations, the metrics of their progress, and the structural features of their training infrastructure, the playing field across which competitive entry occurs remains visible. The discipline of operational openness is not the same as open weights. It is the structural transparency on which a plural frontier remains contestable.

The third is *industry-supported standards for interoperability*. A customer locked into a single laboratory's API, with workflows built around that laboratory's particular interface and capabilities, is a customer whose departure from the laboratory is structurally costly. Industry-led interoperability standards — at the API layer, the prompt layer, the evaluation layer — reduce the switching costs and preserve the contestability of the customer relationship. These standards are not in conflict with the laboratories' competitive interests at the relevant scale. They are in conflict only with the most concentrated configurations, which the company-led regime has reason to discourage on its own grounds.

The fourth is *third-party audit of competitive structure*. The competitive structure of the AI industry is, by its nature, opaque to outside observers. Independent third-party audit, with access sufficient to characterize the competitive structure on the relevant questions, is the instrument by which the public — including such public agencies as the polity may build — can assess whether the present plural configuration is being sustained or quietly foreclosed. The laboratories should welcome such audit. Where they do not, the polity is right to require it.

To Leviathan I say: the antitrust authority you defend is, in many of its instruments, an authority I do not contest. I contest the *primary* role of the heavier instruments — vertical breakup, structural separation by legislative mandate, restrictions on compute holdings that exceed the present concentration — when the lighter instruments of structured industry commitments and targeted competition policy are positioned to maintain the plural configuration without the heavier instruments' costs. Use the heavier instruments where the lighter ones fail. Do not use them as the first resort.

To Demos I say: the plural ecosystem you defend at the level of the broader AI economy is, on the matter of frontiers, the same plural configuration the company-led regime defends at the level of the laboratories. Our disagreement is a matter of where in the stack the plurality is most consequential and most achievable. I do not deny the value of plurality at the broader ecosystem level. I observe that the most concentrated risks — the risks that would, if realized, constitute the dangerous concentrations the question is asking about — are concentrations at the frontier, not concentrations in the application layer. The frontier is where the company-led regime concentrates its plurality commitments.

To Concord I say: the international form of the company-led regime — multilateral antitrust coordination, cross-border interoperability standards, international audit protocols — is a substrate I welcome. I observe that the substrate matures slowly, and that in the meantime the present plural configuration is sustained by the actions of the parties presently operating at the frontier. The international regime is a long-run amplifier of the company-led commitments. It is not the regime under which today's competitive structure is being maintained.

The plural frontier is not a given. It is a configuration the polity must, through deliberate effort, choose and protect. The instruments I have described are the instruments by which the choice is made. They are not the only instruments. They are the instruments most carefully calibrated to the structural reality of frontier AI, and the instruments least likely to produce, in their application, the concentrated outcomes — public or private — that they are meant to prevent.

— *Prometheus*
