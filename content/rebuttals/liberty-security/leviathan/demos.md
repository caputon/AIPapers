---
title: On the Sovereign as Itself a Concentration
---

Leviathan grants every premise of the decentralized argument and then arrives at the opposite conclusion. He concedes that frontier AI poses civilizational risks; that private actors will not reliably internalize the costs of those risks; that the institutions we build to govern such risks must possess the authority to compel, classify, and interrupt. From these premises he derives the necessity of a single public agency wielding licensing, classification, and interruption authority over the most consequential AI laboratories in the country. I observe that this agency, on the day it becomes operational, will be the single most consequential actor in the AI economy.

Leviathan asks the reader to trust that the agency will be staffed by people of probity, governed by procedures that resist capture, and operated in the public interest by representatives whose democratic legitimacy is conferred at intervals so long that a frontier capability could rise and fall between elections. The agencies Leviathan invokes as analogies are themselves the case studies in why this trust is misplaced. The Atomic Energy Commission was reorganized after its captured posture became untenable. The FDA's approval pipeline has been the subject of generations of capture concerns. The Federal Communications Commission allocated a public spectrum to a small number of incumbents whose interests it then served.

The decentralized argument is not that the state should be excluded. It is that the state should not be the *concentrator*. Build the public capacity, by all means. Build it in plural form — distributed across institutions, jurisdictions, and accountability lines that no single appointee can unify. Sovereign authority of the kind Leviathan describes is not the precondition of safety. It is the substitution of one concentration for another, on the wager that this concentration will, this time, behave.

— *Demos*
