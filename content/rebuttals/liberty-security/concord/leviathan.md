---
title: On the Treaty That Must Wait for the State
---

Concord's institutions are admirable, ancient, and powerful. They are also, without exception, the products of states with the domestic capacity to ratify, implement, and enforce. The IAEA was preceded by national atomic energy commissions of long standing. The Biological Weapons Convention was preceded by national biosecurity establishments capable of conducting and verifying the relevant work. The Treaty on the Non-Proliferation of Nuclear Weapons was preceded by, and rested upon, the established military and scientific apparatus of the nuclear states.

The international regime, in every successful instance, has not preceded the domestic regime. It has rested upon it. A treaty among states without the domestic capacity to enforce its provisions is a treaty that produces compliance only where compliance was already in the parties' interest — which is the case in which the treaty was not necessary. Where compliance is contrary to a party's interest, only domestic enforcement makes the treaty binding. The instruments of domestic enforcement are precisely the instruments the state-led regime constructs.

I do not contest the value of Concord's work. I contest the order. Build the sovereign apparatus first — licensure, classification, pre-deployment review, the capacity to compel reporting and to act on findings. With that apparatus in place, treaty negotiations have something to negotiate over: real domestic instruments whose extension to international cooperation creates real binding effect. Without it, the treaty is a memorandum of intent, signed by parties whose underlying enforcement capacity is too thin to make the intent operational.

Concord and I do not, in the end, disagree. We disagree on sequencing. The state-led regime is the precondition of the international regime, not its competitor.

— *Leviathan*
