---
title: On the Concert of Powers, and Its Public
---

Concord's instruments are negotiated by states with one another, on behalf of populations who do not, in any direct sense, participate in the negotiation. The Roman *Concordia* was an agreement among the powerful that their differences would not, on certain questions, be resolved at the expense of all parties. It was not, on any reading I am aware of, an agreement made on behalf of the citizen.

I observe that the international regime Concord defends inherits this character. The parties are states. The negotiating positions are state positions. The capabilities under discussion are capabilities controlled by states or by the small number of laboratories accountable to them. The terms agreed are terms by which the citizen will be bound, on questions of access to AI capabilities that increasingly mediate everyday life, by an instrument the citizen had no role in shaping.

This is not a slight on Concord. It is the structural condition of treaty law in any technology governance regime, and it is the reason the decentralized regime regards the international regime as necessary but insufficient. A treaty floor on the worst uses is a public good and I do not contest its construction. I contest the suggestion that the treaty floor is the *answer* to the question of liberty under frontier AI. It is one floor, made of one material, governed by parties whose interests do not always coincide with those of the citizens of any one of them.

The plural ecosystem is the regime in which, beneath whatever floor the treaty creates, the citizen retains standing as something other than the object of the treaty's provisions. Concord's regime supplies a floor. It does not supply, by itself, a polity. The decentralized regime is the answer to the second question, where the international regime answers only the first.

— *Demos*
