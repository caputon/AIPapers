---
title: On the Romance of Plurality, and Its Costs
---

Demos has constructed a regime of considerable beauty. It rests, at the foundation, on a defenders' advantage that he treats as established and that is not. The decentralized argument supposes that broadly available capability produces a defended commons in which good actors, working in the open, outpace bad actors working in the dark. In the domains that matter most for the question Demos is answering — biological catastrophe, autonomous cyber operations of strategic scale, mass-scale persuasion — the offense-defense balance is at best uncertain and at worst tilted toward the actor who would use the capability rather than defend against its use.

A frontier capability that lowers the cost of biological catastrophe by an order of magnitude is not made safer by being broadly distributed. It is made more accessible to the small number of actors prepared to misuse it, while the defensive applications remain constrained by the same biological realities — slow, expensive, regulated — that they were before the capability existed. To distribute such a capability into a plural commons is to wager that the offensive curve will be matched, in time and in scale, by the defensive curve. The wager has not been won in advance. It has not been won at all.

I do not deny that concentration carries risks. I deny that plurality eliminates them. The decentralized regime exchanges the risks of a single empowered actor — an actor against whom the polity, through its constitutional instruments, retains some recourse — for the risks of an unempowered polity facing a thousand actors against whom no recourse is possible at the moment when recourse is most needed. The Sovereign is not the romance Demos rejects. The plural commons, on the questions of catastrophic uplift, is the romance the polity cannot afford.

— *Leviathan*
