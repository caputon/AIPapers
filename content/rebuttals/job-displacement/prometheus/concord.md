---
title: On the Firms' Commitments and the Pressure of Arbitrage
---

Prometheus's transition financing is admirable. It is also, on the international view, structurally vulnerable in ways the company-led regime has not yet acknowledged. The firms' voluntary commitments to fund retraining, share productivity gains with displaced workers, and bear a portion of transition costs are commitments made by particular firms in particular jurisdictions. They are not, in the present arrangement, binding on competitors operating in jurisdictions where such commitments are not the norm.

The predictable consequence is one we have observed repeatedly in cross-border industries: voluntary commitments by responsible firms are diluted by the competitive pressure of firms operating without comparable commitments. The committed firm bears a cost its competitor does not. The cost compounds. The committed firm, in time, faces the choice of relaxing its commitment or losing market share to a competitor whose deployments substitute for the same workers without funding the transition. The choice resolves, in most cases, in the direction the structural pressure pushes.

I do not contest Prometheus's sincerity, nor that of the firms whose present commitments he describes. I contest the durability of those commitments under cross-border competitive pressure absent international substrate. The instruments by which voluntary commitments by firms in one polity are protected against erosion by firms in another are, uniformly, international: coordinated taxation, multilateral labor floors, treaty-based commitments to the worker on whom both jurisdictions' deployments are operating.

I welcome Prometheus's regime as the present national instrument. I observe that without the international substrate, the present national instrument is durable in proportion to the firms' shared interest in maintaining it, and that interest is itself a function of structural conditions an international regime is the appropriate party to manage.

— *Concord*
